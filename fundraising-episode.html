Fundraising with trust and purpose.

A practical conversation about verifying the recipient, choosing respectful audience groups, setting a transparent goal, protecting family privacy, and reporting where funds go.

The narration uses computer-generated speech. This is general educational information, not legal, tax, medical, or fundraising-compliance advice.

Episode transcript

Welcome to Childhood Cancer Field Guide. This short episode is about planning a trustworthy childhood cancer fundraiser.

Start with a clear purpose and a verified recipient. Before asking anyone to give, confirm the organization’s legal name, official donation page, fees, restrictions, and how gifts may be used.

Set a realistic goal by separating event costs and platform fees from the net funds you hope to deliver. Be clear about gross donations, expenses, and the final transfer. Never promise that a contribution will pay for a particular treatment or outcome unless the recipient confirms it.

Who might be a good audience? Consider people who chose to hear from the organizer, local businesses with community-giving or matching programs, schools and civic groups that approve the activity, peer-to-peer volunteers, and foundations whose published grant criteria fit. These are audience groups, not lists to scrape. Do not use private patient information, infer someone’s ability to give, or continue contacting people who decline.

A respectful campaign explains its purpose, names the verified beneficiary, and makes it easy to ask questions or opt out. Get informed permission before sharing a child’s name, image, diagnosis, or personal story. Keep medical claims accurate; do not promise a cure or imply that a donation guarantees a clinical result.

After the campaign, reconcile donations, fees, and transfers. Thank supporters, provide receipts through the recipient where appropriate, and publish a clear close-out showing what was raised and where it went.

Fundraising can bring a community together when it is transparent, voluntary, and accountable. Verify the recipient, respect privacy, and let families and clinicians guide care decisions.
