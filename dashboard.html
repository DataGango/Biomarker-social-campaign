Childhood cancer research brief

Population estimates are not an individual prognosis.

Childhood cancer includes many distinct diseases. Outcomes vary by diagnosis, age, tumor biology, risk group, and response to treatment; a single overall statistic cannot describe an individual child’s outlook.

Different diseases

Leukemias, brain and central nervous system tumors, lymphomas, and other cancers vary in frequency across age groups.

Leukemia varies

ACS reports approximate overall 5-year survival of about 90% for childhood ALL, 65–70% for AML, and over 92% for CML; subtype and clinical factors matter.

Inherited risk is a subset

NCI estimates inherited pathogenic variants cause about 8–10% of childhood cancers overall; the share differs substantially by cancer type.

Specialist care

Pediatric cancer centers bring expertise in childhood cancers and family-centered care. Clinical-trial participation is voluntary.

Care continues

Some late effects can appear after treatment. Long-term follow-up and a saved treatment summary support ongoing care.

Interpret with context

NCI’s age-specific survival figures refer to 2013–2019. ACS notes that leukemia survival rates are historical estimates, not individual predictions.

Start with the basics

In plain language

What is a biomarker?

A gene, protein, or other substance that can be tested to learn more about a cancer and its features.

What results may help show

A test result is one part of a care discussion.

01

Which treatment might be best for that cancer

02

Whether a cancer is more likely to grow and spread

03

Whether a treatment is working

04

Whether a cancer has come back

The testing journey

A simplified view based on the fact sheet

A sample is collected

Often cancer cells or blood; the sample depends on the cancer and test.

A lab tests the sample

The laboratory checks the sample for selected biomarkers.

Discuss what it means

Ask your cancer care team how the result relates to your situation.

Where samples may come from

Collection method varies by cancer and clinical situation.

Solid tumors

Samples are often taken during a biopsy or surgery. A blood sample may be used when tumor tissue is difficult to obtain.

Blood cancers

Samples may come from blood or bone marrow.

Other sample types

For some cancers, testing may use other body fluids, such as urine.
